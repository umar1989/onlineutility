Ask a question in class and watch what happens: the same few hands go up. If you only call on volunteers, you hear from the same few voices all term. Learning how to pick students fairly in class changes the feel of a lesson, because every student knows they might be asked and prepares accordingly.

## Why fair selection matters

- **Participation is uneven by default.** Confident students answer quickly, while quieter ones wait to be asked, and sometimes never are.
- **Teachers have unconscious favourites.** We look at the students who make eye contact, sit near the front or usually answer correctly.
- **Fairness builds trust.** When students see that selection is truly random, arguments about "why always me?" fade.
- **It improves learning.** A student who may be called on at any moment pays closer attention.

## Common methods and their problems

### Hands up

It is quick and friendly, but it only picks volunteers. Use it for warm-up questions, not as your only method.

### Going down the roll

Roll order is fair, but predictable. Students can work out when their turn will come, relax until then, and switch off afterwards.

### Name sticks or slips

Writing names on sticks in a jar is classic and works well. The downsides are the time needed to make them, the effort of resetting the jar, and the temptation to put aside "difficult" sticks.

### A random picker tool

A digital picker takes the list once and then does everything: random choice, no repeats, resetting. You also project it on a screen, so students see the selection is not decided by you.

## Choosing "no repeat" or "repeat"

- **No repeat until everyone has been picked** is best for fairness across a week or a term. Each student is asked once per cycle.
- **Repeat allowed** is useful for quick quiz games where each round is independent. Nobody is safe, which keeps attention high, although the same student may be chosen twice in a row.

## Make picking feel safe

Random selection works only when students feel safe answering. These habits help:

1. **Warn before you ask.** Say "I will pick someone at random in thirty seconds" and give thinking time.
2. **Allow a pass or a phone-a-friend.** A student who is stuck can name a classmate to help, then you return to them to repeat the answer.
3. **Praise effort, not only correctness.** Thank a student for trying, whatever the answer.
4. **Keep the tone light.** The picker is a game of chance, not a punishment.

## Forming groups randomly

Random groups are also a useful tool. They break cliques and mix abilities without any hint of favouritism.

- Choose either the number of groups or the size of each group.
- Shuffle the whole class and deal students into groups like cards.
- If the numbers do not divide evenly, some groups will have one extra student. That is normal.
- Consider re-shuffling every few weeks so students work with different classmates.

### A worked example

You teach 31 students and want lab groups of about five. Set "students per group" to 5, so the tool creates 7 groups. 31 divided by 7 gives four groups of 4 and three groups of 5, the sizes differing by at most one. If any pair of students should be kept apart or together, you can adjust by hand after the shuffle.

## Keep it private

A class list holds names of real children. Avoid pasting it into websites that store your input. Choose a tool whose list stays in the browser tab, and store your own master list in a document you control. Ours never sends the list anywhere, and refreshing the page clears it.

## Keep a record if you need one

If you grade participation, note who answered and how it went. A random picker does not record marks, so make a quick note in your register right after each pick. Over a term this gives a fair and varied picture of each student.

## Checklist for the next lesson

- List ready and pasted into the picker.
- "No repeat" ticked if you want a full cycle.
- A plan for students who pass.
- A projected screen so the process is visible.
- A quick way to note each student's response.

Fair selection is a small change that gives every student a voice, and it takes almost no effort once your list is in place.
