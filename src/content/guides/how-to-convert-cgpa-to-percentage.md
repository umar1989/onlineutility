Application forms often ask for a percentage, but your result may only show a CGPA. Knowing how to convert CGPA to percentage helps you fill the form correctly and avoid arguments later. The calculation is simple. The tricky part is knowing which rule to apply, and that is what this guide is about.

## What CGPA actually is

CGPA stands for Cumulative Grade Point Average. Each course earns a grade, each grade maps to grade points, and the points are averaged across all courses, weighted by credits. The result is a single number on a fixed scale, most often out of 10, though some institutions use scales out of 4 or 5.

A percentage, by contrast, is a direct ratio of marks obtained to marks possible. A grade point system hides the raw marks behind grade bands, so converting back is always an approximation. A student with 81 marks and a student with 89 marks could both receive the same grade, and therefore the same grade points.

## The two common methods

### Method 1: multiply by a factor

Percentage = CGPA × multiplier

The multiplier is a number set by the board or university. Many institutions use 9.5 for a 10-point scale; some use 10; others publish their own value. For example, with a multiplier of 9.5:

- CGPA 8.2 × 9.5 = 77.9%
- CGPA 7.0 × 9.5 = 66.5%
- CGPA 9.0 × 9.5 = 85.5%

### Method 2: proportional to the scale

Percentage = CGPA ÷ maximum CGPA × 100

On a 10-point scale this is just CGPA × 10, so 8.2 gives 82%. On a 4-point scale a CGPA of 3.2 gives 3.2 ÷ 4 × 100 = 80%.

## So which one is correct?

Whichever your institution says. There is no single universal rule, and we do not claim that any multiplier is official. Look for the rule in these places, in order:

1. The university's website or your marksheet, which often carries a note on conversion.
2. The notification for the exam or admission you are applying to, which may state how to convert.
3. Your institution's examination office, which can issue an equivalence certificate.

If the notification gives its own conversion formula, use that one even if your university uses a different method.

## Converting percentage back to CGPA

Reverse the formula you used. With a multiplier of 9.5, CGPA = percentage ÷ 9.5. For 77.9% that is 77.9 ÷ 9.5 = 8.2. With the proportional method on a 10-point scale, CGPA = percentage ÷ 10.

## A worked example

Anita has a CGPA of 7.8. Her form asks for a percentage to one decimal place.

- Her university states a multiplier of 9.5, so 7.8 × 9.5 = 74.1%.
- If she mistakenly used the proportional method, she would write 78%, which is almost four points higher than her institution's rule gives. On a strict cut-off that gap could decide whether her application is valid.

The lesson: use the number your institution gives, not the one that looks best.

## Rounding

Decide how many decimals you need before you start. Forms usually accept one or two decimals. Keep the full number while you calculate and round only at the end. If the instructions say "do not round up", truncate instead of rounding.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a multiplier you found online without checking it.** Rules change and vary.
- **Mixing scales.** A CGPA out of 4 cannot be multiplied by a factor meant for a 10-point scale.
- **Entering a converted figure where the form wants the original.** If the form has a CGPA field, give the CGPA.
- **Forgetting the semester weights.** Your CGPA already accounts for credits; do not average semester scores yourself unless told to.

## Keep the paperwork

If you need to state a converted percentage, keep a copy of the rule you used, such as the university circular, in case a reviewer asks. A short note with the formula and a screenshot of the source can save a long email chain later.

## Checklist

- Do you know your scale (10, 5 or 4 points)?
- Have you found the official conversion rule for your case?
- Did you apply the correct direction of conversion?
- Is the rounding what the form asks for?
- Does the result look sensible next to your grades?

Once these are answered, the conversion itself takes seconds and your form is on solid ground.
