Percentages appear everywhere in school and in applications: marks, attendance, eligibility cut-offs, discounts. Yet many people are unsure how to calculate percentage of marks without a calculator at hand. The method is short, and once you can do it by hand, you will check any result with confidence.

## What "percent" means

The word comes from the Latin for "per hundred". A percentage is a way of saying how many parts out of 100. So 72% means 72 out of every 100. It lets you compare scores from tests with different totals: 18 out of 25 and 72 out of 100 are the same performance, because both equal 72%.

## The basic formula

**Percentage = (marks obtained ÷ total marks) × 100**

That is all. Divide what you got by what was possible, then multiply by 100.

Example: 432 out of 600.

1. Divide: 432 ÷ 600 = 0.72
2. Multiply by 100: 0.72 × 100 = 72

The answer is 72%. You can try this with the [percentage calculator](/percentage-calculator/) and see the same working written out.

## Percentage across several subjects

When a result has many subjects, add the marks obtained in all subjects, add the maximum marks of all subjects, and use the same formula once. Do not average the individual percentages unless every subject has the same total, because that gives a different answer when totals differ.

Example:

| Subject | Obtained | Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 78 | 100 |
| Science | 64 | 80 |
| English | 41 | 50 |
| Total | 183 | 230 |

Percentage = 183 ÷ 230 × 100 = 79.57%. The average of the three individual percentages (78, 80 and 82) would give 80, which is not the same, because the subjects carry different weights. Check the rule that your school or board uses before you decide which method applies.

## Finding a percent of a number

Sometimes you know the percentage and need the marks. If a paper is out of 80 and you need 45%, compute 45 ÷ 100 × 80 = 36. You need 36 marks.

This is useful for setting targets: "I want 60% overall; how many marks do I need in the last paper?" Work out the total marks needed first, subtract what you already have, and the difference is your target.

## Percentage change: improvement or decline

To compare two results, use the change formula:

**Change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100**

If a student moves from 60 to 72 marks, the change is (72 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = 20%. That is a 20% increase. Note the base is the old value. Using the new value as the base would give a different, wrong answer.

## Percentage points are not the same as percent

If attendance rises from 80% to 90%, it has risen by 10 percentage points, but the relative increase is 10 ÷ 80 = 12.5%. News articles and reports mix these up constantly. When you read or write, say which one you mean.

## Common mistakes

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Use the maximum marks, not the pass marks.
- **Forgetting to multiply by 100.** If you get 0.72, you are one step away.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep extra decimals until the final step, then round once.
- **Mixing up old and new.** The base of a change is always the starting value.
- **Assuming the same rule everywhere.** Boards and universities may round marks or drop the lowest subject in certain cases. Follow the rule printed in your official documents.

## Quick mental tricks

- 10% of any number is that number with the decimal point moved one place left. 10% of 640 is 64.
- 5% is half of 10%. 5% of 640 is 32.
- 1% is the number divided by 100. 1% of 640 is 6.4.
- Build other percentages from these: 15% is 10% plus 5%.

## Check your answer

A fast sanity check saves marks. If you got 183 out of 230, is the result near 80%? Eighty percent of 230 is 184, so 183 should be just under 80, and 79.57 fits. If your result is wildly different, recheck the numbers you typed.

With the formula, a few shortcuts and a habit of checking, percentage questions stop being a worry and become one of the easiest things on the page.
