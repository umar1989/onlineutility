A good seating plan keeps an exam calm. Students know where to sit, invigilators know who should be where, and nobody loses time arguing at the door. If you are wondering how to make an exam hall seating plan that is fair, clear and quick to produce, follow the steps below.

## Start with the room, not the names

Before you touch the student list, understand the room.

1. **Count the usable seats.** Do not count broken benches or seats blocked by pillars, doors or a projector.
2. **Note the layout.** Rows and columns, or paired benches? Are there aisles? A plan that ignores the real layout confuses everyone.
3. **Find the front.** Choose a fixed reference such as the board or the teacher's desk, and always draw the plan from that point of view.
4. **Check the distance rules.** Your school may ask for gaps between students. Decide whether you need every other seat, every other row, or a checkerboard pattern.

Spacing matters more than people expect. With a checkerboard pattern a room of 30 seats holds only 15 students, so you may need two rooms for one class.

## Choose an order that suits the purpose

- **Roll number order** is easiest to check against the attendance list and is good for regular class tests.
- **Snake order** reverses direction on alternate rows, so that nearby roll numbers sit at the row ends close to each other, which helps when handing out and collecting papers.
- **Random order** reduces copying among friends and is a good default for formal tests. Random should really be random, not "random as chosen by a person", which tends to repeat patterns.

When two classes share a room, alternate students from each class so neighbours have different papers or different subjects, if your rules allow it.

### A worked example

Class 9B has 32 students. The room has 6 rows and 6 columns, which is 36 seats, and the school wants every other seat left empty in each row.

- Usable seats: 6 rows × 3 usable seats = 18. That is not enough for 32 students.
- Solution: use two rooms. Seat 16 students in each, keeping the same layout.
- You could also keep one room with a checkerboard pattern per sitting and run the test in two sittings. This takes longer but needs only one room.

Spend a minute doing this calculation before the day. Discovering on the morning of the exam that 14 students have no seat is the stressful way to learn it.

## Label and display the plan

- Print one copy for the door and one for the invigilator.
- Add the room name, subject, date and time at the top.
- Use large, readable names or roll numbers. Students scan quickly and from a distance.
- Mark the front clearly.
- If seats are numbered physically, include those numbers in the plan.

## Handle changes on the day

Absentees leave empty seats. Late arrivals need a place. Keep a few spare seats near the invigilator's desk for late students, so you do not need to reshuffle the plan. If you must change the chart, note the change on the printed copy in pen and date it.

## Fairness and care

Some students need particular seating: those who need to sit near the front because of eyesight or hearing, or those who have a scribe. Place them first, then arrange the others around them. Do this quietly and respectfully, and never announce the reason.

## Privacy

A seating plan usually carries full student names. Do not post it anywhere public online. Choose tools that keep the list on your device, as ours does, and share the printed copy only where needed.

## Checklist

- Seats counted and the layout checked.
- Spacing rules decided.
- Order chosen to suit the test.
- Plan printed, labelled and checked against the attendance list.
- Spare seats and a way to record changes ready.

A few minutes of planning gives you a smooth start to the exam, and you can reuse the same settings for the next test.
